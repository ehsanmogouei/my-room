Every couple of years I make a portfolio site. I pick a font, I write three case studies, I put a grid of rectangles on the home page, and then I abandon it about six weeks later. The pattern is boring enough that I finally sat down and asked why it keeps happening.

The short answer: a portfolio is a shop window, and I do not have anything to sell. I am not looking for clients. I do not want a "let us work together" button at the bottom of every page. What I actually wanted was a place to put things down.

![A desk lamp, a laptop and a mug of cold tea, photographed from above](/gallery/placeholder-01.jpg)

## A room is easier to remember than a grid

The thing that finally clicked for me is that a room has landmarks. A desk means work. A bookshelf means things I have read and written. A wall of frames means images. Once the metaphor was in place, every decision after it got easier, because I was no longer designing a layout, I was furnishing a place.

The layout also stopped being arbitrary. Here is the whole information architecture of this site:

| In the room | On the site | What lives there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Desk | `/projects` | Things I am building or have finished |
| Bookshelf | `/notebook` | Notes, essays, half-formed opinions |
| Gallery wall | `/gallery` | Photographs and screenshots |
| Corkboard | `/gallery#links` | Links I keep coming back to |
| Lamp | everywhere | Switches the room between day and night |
| Door | `/` | A way back out to the rest of the web |

## Three decisions I would make again

### The content is plain files

Everything you are reading is an MDX file with a small frontmatter block. Posts live in `content/posts/fa` and `content/posts/en`, projects in `content/projects`, and the loader reads both folders at build time. There is no database, no CMS, no admin panel, and no draft-mode plugin. A translation is paired with its original by sharing a filename, which is the least clever solution available and therefore the one I have not broken yet.

If a marker in the 3D scene points at something that does not exist, the build fails and prints the file name. All of my frontmatter mistakes get caught before a visitor ever sees them.

### The room is decoration, not the product

The generated artwork is inline SVG rendered on the server, so it never blocks the text and stays sharp at any size. If JavaScript never runs, everything is still there. I like the idea of a site that is a place, but I am not going to hold my writing hostage behind a canvas.

### Markers are just data

The whole interactive layer is one array. Adding something to the room means adding one object to it:

```ts
export const hotspots: Hotspot[] = [
  { id: 'desk', href: '/projects', position: [1.4, 0.72, -0.9], color: '#e8b06a' },
  { id: 'bookshelf', href: '/notebook', position: [-2.1, 1.05, -1.6], color: '#8fb8a0' },
]
```

Clicking a marker flies the camera to it and opens a small panel. Pressing `Escape` puts you back where you started, and the browser back button still works, because the panel is a route and not a modal.

## What I would tell someone about to build one

Make the boring version first. My first attempt was a full 3D apartment with doors that opened and a hallway, and I spent nine evenings on the hallway before writing a single word. The version you are standing in now started as a grey box with two lights and flat text on a plane, and it was ugly for about a week.

The other thing: write the first three posts before you build anything. If you cannot fill a bookshelf, you do not need the bookshelf. You can see what came of mine in [the projects section](/projects) — this site is listed there alongside the others, mostly so I have to look at it.

## Still on the list

- Ambience, but muted by default and never autoplaying
- Better book spines, since close-up they are clearly just boxes
- A keyboard-only path through the whole room
- Testing the scene on a laptop that is older than my career

None of these are urgent. That is sort of the point.
