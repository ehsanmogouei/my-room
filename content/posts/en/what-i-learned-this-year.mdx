Every December I write a list of what I learned, and every December the list is mostly things I already knew, phrased more confidently. This year I kept notes as I went instead, which made the honest version much harder to write and much more useful to reread.

Six things stuck. Three of them are about code.

## What actually stuck

### Boring tools survive contact with me

I rebuilt my terminal configuration from scratch and ended up with six tools, half of which I was already using years ago. The new ones were not the point. Deciding to stop looking was the point, and the eleven months since are the evidence. There is a longer version of this in [my terminal setup](/blog/my-terminal-setup).

### Small releases are a technique, not a virtue

I shipped something to production on most working days this year. The releases that went badly were almost never the small ones. The one that went really badly was a Saturday migration that I had told myself was small. It was not; it changed data and it could not be undone, and I had confused "few lines" with "low risk". I wrote the process notes down in [notes on shipping small](/blog/notes-on-shipping-small).

### Persian typography is a separate education

I built a small playground for comparing Persian typefaces, and it taught me more about the web than most of my work projects did. Line height, letter joining, the way a font that looks fine at 14px falls apart at 11px, and the fact that most of what I knew about vertical rhythm came from Latin text and did not transfer. The playground is in [the projects section](/projects).

## What did not stick

- A daily journal, abandoned in February after forty-one consecutive entries
- A reading tracker that was more fun to build than to use
- Three separate attempts at a personal wiki
- Learning a new language, unless forty minutes of Italian counts

The pattern in that list is that each one needed a habit I did not actually have, and building the tool felt like having the habit. I would like to stop confusing those two things. I probably will not.

> Most of what I call learning is really just noticing something twice.

## The uncomfortable one

The thing I learned that I did not enjoy learning: I am slower than I think I am, and the estimate I give out loud is usually the estimate for the version of me who is not interrupted. This year I started writing down how long things actually took, in a plain text file, one line each.

| Kind of task | My guess | Actual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A small feature, front to back | half a day | 1.5 days |
| Fixing a bug I understand | an hour | 2 hours |
| Anything involving a migration | a day | a week |
| Writing a post like this one | two hours | a day, spread over three |

The numbers are not the point. The point is that I now multiply by two before I say anything out loud, and I have missed fewer things because of it.

## What I want to do differently

Read more code that I did not write, and read it slowly, which I have written about [elsewhere in this notebook](/blog/reading-code-slowly). Finish two projects instead of starting six. Say no to the third thing in a week where two things are already promised.

That is the whole list. It is shorter than last year's, and I am counting that as progress.
